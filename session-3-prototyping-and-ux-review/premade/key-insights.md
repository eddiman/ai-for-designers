# Key insights

> Your work. The insights from the research that the rest of the course builds on. Each one anchored in evidence and labelled as observation or inference.

## Insights
<!-- One insight per entry: the insight, the evidence behind it, and whether it's observed or inferred. -->

### Insight 1
- **Insight:** Make trustworthy arrival status the core experience. The design should help riders quickly answer: is my bus coming, and when will it actually arrive? This should take priority over route discovery or additional features.
- **Evidence:**
  - Observed, said: Maja, Geir and Sofie all describe uncertainty or inaccurate information as worse than the delay itself.
  - Observed, measured: "Not knowing if the bus is actually coming" was the leading questionnaire frustration at 31%; incorrect real-time information accounted for another 17%.
  - Observed, measured: Only 23% trust the real-time information they receive, while 55% distrust it.
  - Observed, measured: 47% selected accurate real-time arrival information as their single most important improvement, and 79% agree with "I just want to reliably know when my bus will arrive, without needing extra features."
- **Observed / inferred:** Inferred. The evidence is said in the interviews and measured in the questionnaire, and the priority it sets is an inference.
- **Design implication:** Show the vehicle's current status and best available arrival estimate prominently, rather than presenting scheduled times as though they are live information.

### Insight 2
- **Insight:** Communicate disruption states clearly. The experience must clearly distinguish between on time, delayed, uncertain, diverted and cancelled. Riders need enough information to decide whether to wait, leave or choose another option.
- **Evidence:**
  - Observed, said: Maja says riders receive too little guidance during disruptions.
  - Observed, said: Geir waited more than 20 minutes for a bus that never arrived because no disruption information was provided.
  - Observed, said: Sofie cannot tell whether a bus is delayed or cancelled.
  - Observed, said: Geir wants clear messages when buses are delayed, cancelled or diverted.
  - Observed, measured: The questionnaire contains 16 mentions of clearer disruption and cancellation communication and 22 mentions of reducing waiting without information.
  - Observed, said: One questionnaire response states, "If it's cancelled, tell me."
- **Observed / inferred:** Inferred. The evidence is said in all three interviews and measured in the questionnaire's open-text answers, and the states the experience must distinguish are an inference from it.
- **Design implication:** When certainty is low, communicate that uncertainty explicitly instead of displaying a precise-looking countdown that may be wrong. Alternative actions should be shown when reliable information is available, but alternative-route suggestions remain a research gap rather than a validated requirement.

### Insight 3
- **Insight:** Design for fast checks of journeys riders already know. For familiar journeys, riders generally do not need help finding the correct route. They need a fast way to check the operational status of a route they already know.
- **Evidence:**
  - Observed, said: Maja takes the same routes daily and uses her own experience to judge reliability.
  - Observed, said: Geir regularly takes Bus 9 from the same stop and knows the route.
  - Observed, said: Sofie relies on her existing knowledge on familiar journeys.
  - Inferred, in the questionnaire summary: The summary concludes that passengers generally know which bus they need, and that the difficult part is knowing whether and when it will arrive. This is the summary's conclusion, not a measured figure.
  - Observed, said: Maja and Sofie build extra time into their journeys because they cannot rely on the information.
- **Observed / inferred:** Inferred. The interview evidence is observed, and the questionnaire point is the summary's own conclusion.
- **Design implication:** The default experience should offer immediate access to nearby or familiar departures, with minimal searching and as few steps as possible. Route planning can remain available for unfamiliar journeys, but should not dominate routine use.

### Insight 4 (optional)
- **Insight:** Keep personalisation and notifications optional. Personalisation may help a meaningful minority, but it should not be required to receive the essential arrival and disruption information.
- **Evidence:**
  - Observed, said: Maja wants saved routes, routine recognition and proactive notifications.
  - Observed, said: Geir does not want accounts, setup, extra features or push notifications.
  - Observed, said: Sofie wants important cancellation or disruption alerts, but not many notifications or extensive personalisation.
  - Observed, measured: Only 14% chose a personalised app as their single preferred improvement.
  - Observed, measured: 34% would use an app with favourites and notifications, 38% would not, and 28% were neutral.
- **Observed / inferred:** Inferred. The evidence is said and measured, and the conclusion that personalisation should be optional is an inference.
- **Design implication:** Provide a simple no-setup experience first. Saved routes, routine learning and alerts should be opt-in layers, with controls over what triggers a notification. The evidence supports urgent disruption alerts more strongly than broad, frequent notifications.

### Insight 5 (optional)
- **Insight:** Provide consistent information across phones and bus stops. The solution should not depend exclusively on a smartphone app. The same reliable status information should be available through both digital and physical channels.
- **Evidence:**
  - Observed, said: Geir primarily uses stop timetables and display boards and wants them to work accurately without installation or setup.
  - Observed, said: Maja prefers an app-based experience, while Sofie wants phone alerts for important disruptions.
  - Observed, said: Sofie describes her grandmother as relying on signage and help from drivers. This is second-hand.
  - Observed, measured: 39% use an app, 27% use printed timetables, and 18% use stop displays.
  - Observed, measured: Together, printed timetables and stop displays make 45% who rely on information at the stop. The triangulation adds the two figures; the questionnaire summary reports them separately.
- **Observed / inferred:** Inferred. The channel use is observed, and the need for consistent information across channels is an inference.
- **Design implication:** Treat the app and stop display as interfaces to the same service status, using consistent arrival estimates, cancellation messages and terminology. Accessibility needs are important but remain under-researched because they were raised only indirectly and were not measured in the questionnaire.
