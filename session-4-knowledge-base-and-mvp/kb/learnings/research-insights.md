# Research insights

## Trust in real-time information is the primary problem

The interview participants describe incorrect countdowns, ghost buses, and unreliable arrival predictions. The questionnaire reports that 55% do not trust real-time information. Its three timing-related frustration categories total about 74%: not knowing if the bus is coming (31%), schedule mismatch (26%), and incorrect real-time information (17%).

**Implication recorded in the artifacts:** Prioritize information that riders can believe is accurate enough to act on.

## Uncertainty causes more pain than delay

Interviewees describe not knowing as worse than waiting, and associate uncertainty with stress and poor decisions. The questionnaire reports that 79% mainly want to reliably know when the bus will arrive.

**Implication recorded in the artifacts:** Help people decide what to do under uncertainty, rather than only show a countdown.

## Riders compensate for unreliable information

The interviews describe cross-checking sources, leaving early, taking earlier buses, and relying on memory or physical displays. These workarounds add time, effort, and stress.

**Implication recorded in the artifacts:** A solution should reduce checking, guessing, and the need to compensate for the system.

## Assistance needs differ

Maja wants proactive, personalized help. Geir wants simple, reliable basics. Sofie wants simplicity on familiar trips and more guidance for unfamiliar or disrupted journeys. The questionnaire's mixed attitude to new apps reinforces the need not to assume a feature-heavy app is the answer.

**Implication recorded in the artifacts:** Keep trusted basics central; layer assistance only where it is useful.

## Research tension and limits

The interviews reveal behavior, emotion, cognitive load, and varied preferences. The questionnaire provides scale and prioritization. It also raises service reliability and frequency (21%) as a meaningful secondary priority, which the interviews emphasize less.
