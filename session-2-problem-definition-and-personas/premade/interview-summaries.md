## Interview 1: Maja (daily commuter, no car)

### Generic summary
**Participant:** Maja, 29, marketing coordinator. She has no car and uses the bus daily for commuting and errands. The interview was recorded on 2026-05-20 (about 35 minutes, semi-structured; this is an excerpt).

**Overview:** Maja relies on the bus for almost everything. Her morning commute has two legs, the 12 into the centre and then the 4 to the office. Her evening trips change from night to night depending on the gym or meeting people. Most of the interview covers how much effort it takes her to plan trips with today's tools and what she would want from a better app.

**How she plans trips today:**
- She checks the transit app first, then the live map because she does not fully trust the app, and often just leaves early and hopes.
- She keeps a mental version of the timetable. Maja knows "the :08 is a liar," so she aims for the :23 bus but leaves as if she were catching the :08. She says it is "exhausting when you say it out loud."
- She makes the same trips repeatedly, but the app makes her type the route in fresh every time. In Maja's words: "I've done this exact journey four hundred times."

**Pain points:**
- **Generic apps:** Every app she has tried opens with a blank search box and a map of the whole region, and none of them remember anything between sessions. Maja calls it "like having an assistant with amnesia."
- **Unreliable live information:** Her worst experience was standing at the stop in heavy rain while the sign showed 2 minutes for about fifteen minutes, after which the bus disappeared from the sign entirely. She did not know whether to keep waiting or run for the train. Maja describes "That feeling of being *stuck guessing*" as the worst part.
- **Not knowing matters more than delay:** Maja says, "I don't even mind the bus being late. I mind not knowing." and "Late I can plan around if you tell me. Silence is what kills me."
- **Service capacity:** She would like more frequent buses, and the 4 is so full around 5pm that she sometimes cannot get on. She sees these as infrastructure problems that are unlikely to change and thinks the information side is where effort should go.

**What she wants:**
- A personal app with saved routes (home, work, gym, her mum's) and favourites.
- Proactive notifications, such as "leave now" based on her location and live traffic, and warnings before she reaches the stop that her usual bus is delayed, full or cancelled, together with the next option.
- Control over when she is notified, for example weekday mornings only and nothing on Sundays.
- Possibly calendar integration, so an early meeting prompts her to leave earlier.
- She says she would pay for an app like this.

**Her ideal scenario:** She opens her phone and it already knows it is Tuesday and she is going to the office. It tells her to leave in 4 minutes, take the 12 then the 4, that she will arrive at 8:52, and that the 4 is a bit full today. She taps once, gets a nudge if anything changes, and never types a route again.

**Key takeaways:**
- Maja's main frustration is uncertainty: live information she cannot trust and a planning routine she has to keep in her head every morning.
- She sees the solution as one personalised app that learns her routines and notifies her proactively. As Maja puts it, "Personalisation is the whole game for me."
- Her worst moment, the bus vanishing from the sign in the rain, was about missing information rather than the delay itself, and she draws that distinction explicitly.
- She wants the app to feel like her own. Maja says: "I want something that's learned my life and has my back."

### Directed summary
Interview Summary: Maja (29, Daily Bus User)
How She Catches the Bus Today

Maja relies entirely on public transport and uses the bus multiple times a day for commuting and errands. Her typical commute involves taking the 12 bus from home to the city centre and then transferring to the 4 bus to reach her office.

To decide when to leave, she:

Checks a transit app.
Sometimes checks a live bus map.
Relies heavily on her own experience of which buses are usually reliable.
Often leaves earlier than necessary because she does not fully trust the information provided.

She has developed personal workarounds and mental rules for managing her commute, especially around buses that frequently deviate from their schedules.

What Frustrates Her
Uncertainty and Poor Real-Time Information

Maja's biggest frustration is not delays themselves, but not knowing what is happening. When a bus is late, disappears from displays, or fails to arrive, she is left guessing whether to keep waiting or find another option.

She feels that transport systems provide insufficient guidance during disruptions and leave riders to figure things out themselves.

Too Much Mental Effort

She feels she is constantly managing her bus journey manually, even though she takes the same routes every day. She must repeatedly check schedules, compare information sources, and make judgment calls about when to leave.

This creates unnecessary cognitive load for routine trips.

Generic Transit Apps

Current apps feel designed for occasional users rather than regular commuters. Every time she opens them, she has to search for journeys again instead of seeing the routes she uses most often.

She feels that existing apps:

Forget her preferences.
Fail to learn her routines.
Provide the same generic experience to everyone.
Lack of Personalisation

Maja repeatedly emphasizes that bus apps do not adapt to her life. She wants technology that understands her habits and helps manage her daily travel instead of requiring her to start from scratch each time.

What She Wishes Existed
A Personalised Bus Assistant

Maja wants an app that learns her travel patterns and proactively helps her travel.

Key features she described include:

Saved favourite routes such as home, work, gym, and family.
Automatic recognition of her regular journeys.
One-tap access to common trips.
An interface centered on her most-used routes rather than a generic search screen.
Smart, Proactive Notifications

She wants the app to tell her when action is needed rather than forcing her to constantly check schedules.

Examples include:

"Leave now for your usual bus."
"Your regular bus is delayed."
"Take this alternative route."
Notifications about cancellations or overcrowding before she reaches the stop.

She also wants control over when and how often notifications are sent.

Context-Aware Travel Planning

Maja imagines an app that:

Knows her daily routines.
Understands the day of the week.
Integrates with her calendar.
Adapts recommendations based on meetings, traffic, delays, and bus occupancy.
Key Takeaway

Maja wants a highly personalised transit app that learns her routines, proactively manages disruptions, and removes the uncertainty and mental effort involved in daily bus travel. Her primary pain point is not the bus service itself, but having to constantly monitor, interpret, and manage travel information on her own.

### Differences analysis
Both summaries describe the same interview and agree on the main points. Maja makes the same trips every day, she does not trust the live information, and she wants an app that knows her routes and tells her when to leave. They differ in how much of the transcript they keep, how close they stay to her own words, and what they put first.

**Structure:**
- The first summary follows the interview in this order: participant details, an overview, how she plans trips, pain points, what she wants, her ideal scenario, and takeaways.
- The second summary is sorted under the three themes from your second prompt: how she catches the bus, what frustrates her, and what she wishes existed. Most of what falls outside those themes is left out.

**Specifics and quotes:**
The first summary keeps the specific details and quotes Maja several times. The second summary turns the same material into general statements, and none of its quoted text matches the transcript word for word. For example:
- The :08 and :23 workaround becomes "personal workarounds and mental rules ... especially around buses that frequently deviate from their schedules."
- The rain incident, where the sign showed 2 minutes for about fifteen minutes and then the bus disappeared from it, becomes "When a bus is late, disappears from displays, or fails to arrive."
- Her ideal Tuesday morning (leave in 4 minutes, the 12 then the 4, in at 8:52, the 4 a bit full today) becomes a general list under "Context-Aware Travel Planning."

Because the first summary quotes her, you can search the transcript for each claim. To check the second summary, you have to reread the transcript.

**What the second summary leaves out:**
- **Crowding and frequency:** Maja would like more frequent buses, and she says the 4 is so full at 5pm that she sometimes cannot get on. That is a frustration about catching the bus, so it fits the second prompt, but it is missing; the second summary mentions overcrowding only as something she wants to be notified about. The second summary then concludes that her primary pain point "is not the bus service itself." A reader cannot see that she did raise a service problem and then set it aside herself as something that will not change.
- **Evening trips:** Her evening route changes depending on the gym or who she is meeting. The first summary mentions this. The second describes her as taking "the same routes every day."
- **Willingness to pay:** She says she would pay for an app like this.
- **Interview context:** Her occupation, the recording date and length, and the fact that the transcript is an excerpt.

**What the second summary adds or states more strongly:**
- **Quotes that are not hers:** The notification examples are in quotation marks, but "Take this alternative route." is not something Maja said. Her words were "here's the next one" and "here's your next option." The other two examples are rephrased from her wording. A reader would take all three as her words.
- **Calendar:** Maja said "Maybe it knows my calendar." The first summary keeps this as "Possibly." The second lists "Integrates with her calendar" next to the things she was sure about, and nothing marks it as uncertain.
- **Notification control:** The second summary says she wants control over "when and how often" notifications are sent. She talked about when (weekday mornings only, nothing on Sundays) and said nothing about how often.
- **General claims:** "She feels that transport systems provide insufficient guidance during disruptions" is the summary's own wording. Maja talked about her app, her stop and the sign she relies on.

**What each summary puts first:**
- The first summary opens its takeaways with uncertainty. It points out that her worst moment was about missing information, which she separates from the delay: "I don't even mind the bus being late. I mind not knowing." It presents the personal app as her proposed solution ("She sees the solution as one personalised app").
- The second summary includes the not-knowing point too, as its first frustration. However, two of its four frustrations are about apps being generic and not personalised, and its wishes read as a feature list. Its final takeaway also opens with "Maja wants a highly personalised transit app." The result is that her proposed solution comes before the problem she described.

**What this means for using them:**
Use the first summary when you need to trace a claim back to the transcript. The second summary is quicker to scan by theme. Before you use it, add the crowding on the 4 back in, replace the quoted notification examples with her own words, and mark the calendar idea as a maybe. In both summaries, keep her problem (not knowing whether her bus is coming) separate from her proposed fix (a personal app). The feature list shows what one person imagines, while the problem is something you can compare with your other interviews.

## Interview 2: Geir (occasional rider, drives otherwise)


### Generic summary
Interview Summary: Geir (54, Warehouse Supervisor)
How he catches the bus today
Geir uses the bus about three times a week, primarily for trips into the city centre for errands, appointments, or the market.
He normally drives elsewhere but chooses the bus to avoid expensive and difficult parking in town.
His travel pattern is highly routine: he takes the same route (Bus 9) from the same stop at roughly the same times.
He does not make complex journeys or transfers.
To check departures, he mainly relies on the timetable at the stop and electronic display boards rather than mobile apps.
Main frustrations

1. Unreliable real-time information

His biggest frustration is not delays themselves, but inaccurate information about delays.
Electronic signs frequently display arrival times that change unexpectedly or show buses that never arrive.
This creates a lack of trust in the information system.

"It's not the waiting, the not knowing whether to trust the thing in front of you."

2. Lack of communication during disruptions

During diversions, roadworks, or service interruptions, he feels passengers are left uninformed.
He described waiting more than 20 minutes for a bus that never came because no clear disruption information was provided.
He would have made different travel decisions if accurate disruption information had been available.

3. Bus apps feel overly complicated

He tried using a bus app but found the setup burdensome.
Creating accounts, configuring favourites, enabling notifications, and other features felt unnecessary for his simple use case.
He feels many transport apps are designed for heavy transit users rather than occasional riders like himself.

4. Notification overload

He actively dislikes push notifications and additional digital interruptions.
Features such as saved routes and alerts provide little value because he regularly travels the same route and already knows it well.
What he wishes existed

Simple, accurate, trustworthy information

Geir repeatedly emphasizes that he does not want more features.
He wants existing information channels, such as stop displays and timetables, to be accurate and honest.

Specifically, he wants:

Reliable countdowns that reflect actual arrival times.
Clear messages when buses are delayed, cancelled, or diverted.
Straightforward access to essential information without accounts, setup, or notifications.
A system that prioritizes accuracy over extra functionality.
Key Insight

For Geir, the problem is trust, not technology. He is not seeking sophisticated journey-planning tools, personalization, or alerts. As a habitual and occasional bus user, he values simple, low-effort access to dependable information. His ideal experience is one where passengers are clearly informed about what is actually happening so they can make their own decisions.

Design Implications
Prioritize accuracy and transparency of real-time information over adding new features.
Clearly communicate disruptions, cancellations, and diversions at stops and in digital channels.
Provide low-friction information access that does not require accounts or extensive setup.
Consider the needs of occasional, routine riders, not only frequent public transport users.
Build trust by explaining uncertainty rather than presenting inaccurate certainty.

Core quote:

"Make the information you already give people actually true. Forget the bells and whistles."

## Interview 3: Sofie (student, mixed-mode traveller)

### Generic summary

Summary: Interview with Sofie (22)
How she catches the bus today
Sofie uses the bus a few times a week, mainly as a backup when walking or cycling is not practical due to rain, being late, or travelling at night after work.
She rarely plans trips far in advance. Instead, she opens a transit app shortly before leaving and checks the next departure.
Because of previous negative experiences, she no longer fully trusts the departure times shown in the app.
To reduce risk, she deliberately catches earlier buses than necessary and builds extra time into her journeys.
What frustrates her

Reliability and trust are by far her biggest frustrations.

She experienced a "ghost bus" before an important exam: the app showed a bus approaching, the countdown reached "due," but the bus never arrived.
Since that incident, she has lost confidence in the accuracy of real-time information.
The most stressful part is not waiting itself, but uncertainty. She cannot tell whether a bus is slightly delayed or has been cancelled entirely.
This uncertainty creates ongoing anxiety and causes her to overcompensate by leaving earlier than needed.
She feels that inaccurate information has a larger impact than the actual delay because it prevents her from making informed decisions.
What she wishes existed

1. Accurate, trustworthy arrival information

Her primary request is simple: departure times that are honest and reliable.
She believes that if she could trust the times displayed in the app or at stops, her entire experience with public transport would improve.

2. Cancellation and disruption alerts

She wants proactive notifications when a bus is cancelled or not running.
She specifically wants alerts for events that would affect her journey, rather than generic notifications.

3. Better guidance for unfamiliar journeys

While she wants a simple experience in her everyday travel, her needs change when travelling in unfamiliar cities.
On these trips, she would like more guidance around finding the correct stop, direction, and route.
What she does not want
She is not interested in highly personalized transit apps with profiles, saved routes, complex setup, or frequent notifications.
She believes she would ignore most advanced features and disable excessive notifications.
Her view is that transit services should prioritize reliability before adding sophisticated features.
Accessibility observations
Through her grandmother's experience, she recognizes that some passengers have very different needs.
Her grandmother struggles with physical accessibility, identifying the correct stop, and would not use a smartphone app.
This made Sofie aware that public transport must support both digitally confident users and passengers who rely on physical signage and staff assistance.
Key insight

Sofie's experience is defined by trust. She does not want more features, personalization, or engagement. She wants confidence that the information she receives is accurate and timely. For her, a simple system that reliably reports arrivals and cancellations would deliver far more value than a feature-rich transit app.
